## Steelman & Primary Source Readback (`Becker et al., 2025-07-10 & METR, 2026-02-24`)

Whereas observational vendor studies frequently compare developers who self-select into AI coding tools against non-adopters across unmatched tasks, Becker et al. (`arXiv:2507.09089`, `2025-07-10`, Sections 2-4) implemented a within-maintainer, issue-level randomized controlled trial. Across `16` experienced open-source maintainers working on mature repositories where they held multi-year commit history (averaging `> 22,000` GitHub stars), `246` real maintenance issues were time-forecasted prior to assignment and randomized `1:1` to `AI-allowed` (primarily `Cursor Pro` with `Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet`) versus `AI-disallowed` control, with active work time logged and verified against screen recordings. Importantly, Becker et al. reported developer-clustered uncertainty intervals alongside explicit scope caveats warning against extrapolating from high-context repository maintainers to greenfield or novice settings. In its subsequent `2026-02-24` uplift-design update, METR documented that developers increasingly declined participation or control-arm compliance when barred from AI assistants, and chose not to publish selection-confounded naive speedup point estimates.

Direct task execution did not calibrate subjective perception: on the signed percentage-change scale, post-task developer beliefs (`-20.0%`) differed from the randomized `ITT` estimate (`+19.0%`) by `+39.0` percentage points (and on the multiplier ratio scale, `1.19 / 0.80 = 1.4875x`). Note that `N = 16` maintainers imposes substantial cluster-level sampling variance (`95% CI` spanning roughly `+2%` to `+39%`), and self-timed work logs verified via screen capture remain subject to standard human-factors effort variation—limitations Becker et al. explicitly acknowledge rather than conceal.

### Witness 2: Illustrative Potential-Outcomes Sensitivity Model for Selective Control Attrition (`[S2]`, `[S3]`)

To illustrate algebraically why dropping non-compliant `No-AI` control tasks invalidates naive as-treated comparisons—supporting METR's `2026-02-24` methodological caution—consider a simple one-sided attrition sensitivity model evaluated in `reproduce_hieh_witnesses.py` (`[S2]`, `[S3]`):

1. Suppose true population potential completion times satisfy `E[T(1)] = E[T(0)] = 1.00` across the randomized issue pool (an exact `0.00%` true average `ITT` effect).
2. Suppose `AI-allowed` assignments (`Z = 1`) achieve `100%` retention (`E[T | Z = 1, retained] = 1.00`), whereas in the `No-AI` control arm (`Z = 0`), developers selectively decline or cross over on `alpha = 35.0%` of tasks that happen to be shorter, routine issues with mean normalized duration `0.60`, leaving `1 - alpha = 65.0%` retained compliant control tasks.
3. By expectation decomposition `1.00 = 0.35 * 0.60 + 0.65 * E[T(0) | retained]`, the retained compliant control subgroup has mean duration `(1.00 - 0.21) / 0.65 = 1.2154` because fast tasks were selectively removed from the control denominator while remaining in the `AI-allowed` denominator.
4. A naive per-protocol comparison of retained `AI-allowed` (`1.00`) against retained compliant `No-AI` (`1.2154`) yields `1.00 / 1.2154 = 0.8228`—an **illusory `-17.72%` speedup solely from asymmetric fast-task attrition under a `0.00%` true effect**.

While this `35%` / `0.60x` calculation is a stylized illustrative sensitivity model rather than an empirical measurement of METR's `2026` cohort, it demonstrates cleanly why METR's refusal to report unadjusted per-protocol speedup numbers adheres to sound causal inference (`OUTCOME_1_ORIGINAL_CLAIM_SUSTAINED`).
